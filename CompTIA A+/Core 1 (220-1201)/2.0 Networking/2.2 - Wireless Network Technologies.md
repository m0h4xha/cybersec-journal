# 2.2 - Wireless Network Technologies
 

## Main Idea

Wireless technologies allow devices to communicate without a physical cable.

The most common wireless technologies covered in this video are:

-   **Wi-Fi (802.11)**

-   **Bluetooth**

-   **RFID**

-   **NFC**

Each technology is designed for a different purpose.

The most important concepts to understand are:

-   Wi-Fi standards and naming

-   Wireless frequency bands

-   Wi-Fi channels

-   Channel bandwidth

-   Wi-Fi interference

-   Bluetooth range and frequency

-   RFID tags and how they receive power

-   NFC and its relationship to RFID

---

## Important Concepts

### 1\. IEEE 802.11 and Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi networks are standardized by the **IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers)**.

The wireless networking standards are part of the **802.11** family.

That's why Wi-Fi networks are often called **802.11 networks**.

Instead of remembering names such as 802.11ac or 802.11ax, modern Wi-Fi generations use simpler names:

| IEEE Standard | Wi-Fi Name |
| --- | --- |
| **802.11ac** | **Wi-Fi 5** |
| **802.11ax** | **Wi-Fi 6** |
| **802.11ax (6 GHz extension)** | **Wi-Fi 6E** |
| **802.11be** | **Wi-Fi 7** |

### Why the Wi-Fi naming system exists

The traditional IEEE names contain letters that can be difficult to remember.

For example:

`802.11ac`

is now commonly referred to as:

**Wi-Fi 5**

This makes it easier to identify different generations of Wi-Fi.

---

## 2\. Wi-Fi Frequency Bands

Wi-Fi can operate across several frequency ranges.

The major bands discussed in this video are:

-   **2.4 GHz**

-   **5 GHz**

-   **6 GHz**

Some access points and wireless devices can operate on multiple frequency bands simultaneously.

### General idea

Different Wi-Fi standards and devices can use different frequency ranges.

The amount of available spectrum affects things such as:

-   Number of available channels

-   Interference

-   Available bandwidth

-   Potential throughput

---

## 3\. Wi-Fi Channels

Instead of constantly referring to exact frequency values, Wi-Fi divides the available spectrum into **channels**.

A channel provides an easier way to identify a particular portion of the wireless spectrum.

For example:

-   2.4 GHz **Channel 6** corresponds to approximately **2.437 GHz**

-   5 GHz **Channel 44** corresponds to approximately **5.220 GHz**

So instead of saying:

> "I'm communicating at 5.220 GHz"

you can simply say:

> "I'm using 5 GHz Channel 44."

### Why channels are useful

Channels make wireless configuration easier because they provide standardized identifiers for portions of the frequency spectrum.

You can see the channel being used by checking the wireless configuration on a device.

---

## 4\. Wireless Bandwidth

**Bandwidth** refers to the amount of spectrum being used for a wireless communication.

Common Wi-Fi channel bandwidths include:

-   **20 MHz**

-   **40 MHz**

-   **80 MHz**

-   **160 MHz**

Generally, using a wider channel can provide higher potential throughput because more spectrum is available for transmitting data.

However, wider channels also consume more of the available spectrum.

---

## 5\. 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi

The **2.4 GHz** band has relatively limited available spectrum.

This means there are fewer opportunities to use non-overlapping channels.

In the video, three 20 MHz channel ranges are shown that do not overlap:

-   Channel 1

-   Channel 6

-   Channel 11

These are commonly used as the three non-overlapping 20 MHz channels in the 2.4 GHz band.

### Why interference can be a problem

Because there is relatively little spectrum available in 2.4 GHz, many nearby wireless networks and devices may compete for the same frequencies.

This can result in:

-   Wireless interference

-   Reduced performance

-   Lower throughput

This is especially noticeable in environments with many nearby wireless networks.

---

## 6\. 5 GHz Wi-Fi

The **5 GHz** band provides considerably more available spectrum than 2.4 GHz.

This means there are more frequencies and channels available.

5 GHz can also use wider channel bandwidths such as:

-   20 MHz

-   40 MHz

-   80 MHz

-   160 MHz

Using wider channels can increase potential throughput.

### General advantage

Compared with 2.4 GHz:

**5 GHz → More available spectrum → More channels → More options for wider bandwidth**

---

## 7\. 6 GHz Wi-Fi

The **6 GHz** band provides even more available spectrum.

This gives Wi-Fi networks:

-   More available frequencies

-   More available channels

-   More opportunities for wider bandwidths

The larger amount of spectrum makes 6 GHz useful for modern high-performance wireless networking.

---

## 8\. Bluetooth

Bluetooth is another wireless technology, but it is different from Wi-Fi.

Bluetooth is commonly used for personal devices such as:

-   Wireless headsets

-   Wireless speakers

-   Keyboards

-   Mice

-   Other personal peripherals

### Bluetooth Frequency

Bluetooth operates in the:

**2.4 GHz range**

This is part of the unlicensed **ISM band**.

---

## 9\. ISM Band

**ISM** stands for:

**Industrial, Scientific and Medical**

The ISM bands are unlicensed frequency ranges that can be used by devices without requiring a specialized radio license.

This is one reason technologies such as:

-   Wi-Fi

-   Bluetooth

can be used by consumers without obtaining an individual radio-frequency license.

---

## 10\. Bluetooth Range

Bluetooth is generally designed for shorter-range personal connectivity than Wi-Fi.

For typical consumer Bluetooth devices, the video gives a maximum range of approximately:

**10 meters**

This makes Bluetooth appropriate for devices that are physically close to the user.

### Example

A phone can connect to:

```text
Phone
  ↓
Bluetooth
  ↓
Wireless Headset
```

The devices don't need to communicate across an entire building like a Wi-Fi network might.

### Wi-Fi vs Bluetooth

| Feature | Wi-Fi | Bluetooth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary purpose | Network connectivity | Personal device connectivity |
| Typical range | Larger | Shorter |
| Frequency | 2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz | 2.4 GHz |
| Example | Internet/network access | Headset/speaker |

---

## 11\. RFID — Radio Frequency Identification

**RFID** stands for:

**Radio Frequency Identification**

RFID is used to identify and track objects using radio frequencies.

Common applications include:

-   Building access badges

-   Product tags

-   Manufacturing/assembly lines

-   Pet identification

### RFID Example: Pet Identification

A tiny RFID tag can be implanted in a pet.

The tag can be approximately the size of a grain of rice.

When the pet is scanned, the scanner can retrieve the tag's identification number and use a database to determine which pet the ID belongs to.

---

## 12\. Passive RFID Tags

A common RFID tag does not have its own battery.

It normally remains inactive without an external source of power.

When an RFID scanner is brought close to the tag:

1.  The scanner produces a radio-frequency signal.

2.  The RFID tag receives energy from that signal.

3.  The tag uses that energy to power its circuitry.

4.  The tag sends its identification information back to the scanner.

5.  The scanner uses the ID to look up information in a database.

This type of RFID tag is called a **passive RFID tag**.

### Key Concept

**Passive RFID → No internal battery → Powered by the scanner's RF energy**

---

## 13\. Active RFID Tags

Some RFID tags contain their own battery.

These are **active RFID tags**.

Because they have their own power source, they can generally be detected from a greater distance than passive tags.

### Passive vs Active RFID

| Feature | Passive RFID | Active RFID |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internal battery | No | Yes |
| Power source | RF energy from scanner | Internal battery |
| Typical range | Shorter | Longer |
| Complexity | Lower | Higher |

---

## 14\. NFC — Near-Field Communication

**NFC** stands for:

**Near-Field Communication**

NFC is commonly found in modern mobile devices such as:

-   Smartphones

-   Smartwatches

NFC builds on technology related to RFID.

### RFID vs NFC

Traditional RFID is generally associated with **one-way identification/communication**.

NFC extends this concept to support **two-way communication** between compatible devices.

This allows devices to exchange information when they are brought very close together.

---

## 15\. Common NFC Uses

### Contactless Payments

One of the most common uses of NFC is mobile payment.

For example:

```text
Smartphone / Smartwatch
          ↓
         NFC
          ↓
Point-of-Sale Terminal
```

You can use your phone or smartwatch to pay for products at a compatible payment terminal.

### Device Configuration

NFC can also help configure other wireless devices.

For example, when setting up a device that needs to connect to Wi-Fi or Bluetooth, NFC can be used to transfer configuration information from a mobile phone to the device.

### Identification and Access

NFC can also be used for:

-   Door access

-   Identification

-   Other short-range authentication/access applications

### Understanding the Wireless Technologies Together

Think of the technologies based on their intended use:

```text
Wi-Fi
│
├── Network connectivity
├── Internet access
└── Larger-area wireless networking

Bluetooth
│
├── Personal devices
├── Headsets
├── Speakers
└── Short-range connectivity

RFID
│
├── Identification
├── Tracking
├── Access badges
└── Product/pet tags

NFC
│
├── Very short-range communication
├── Contactless payments
├── Identification/access
└── Device configuration
```

---

### Frequency vs Channel vs Bandwidth

These three terms are related but different.

#### Frequency

The actual radio frequency being used.

Example:

**2.437 GHz**

#### Channel

A standardized identifier for a portion of the wireless spectrum.

Example:

**Channel 6**

#### Bandwidth

The amount of spectrum being used for the communication.

Example:

**20 MHz**

So a Wi-Fi configuration could be described as:

> 2.4 GHz band → Channel 6 → 20 MHz bandwidth

---

### Wi-Fi Spectrum Comparison

A useful way to think about the three major Wi-Fi bands:

| Band | Available Spectrum | Channel Options | Interference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **2.4 GHz** | Limited | Fewer | More likely |
| **5 GHz** | More | More | Generally less crowded |
| **6 GHz** | Much more | Many more | More available spectrum |

The exact real-world behavior depends on the environment and devices, but the important A+ concept is that **higher bands such as 5 GHz and 6 GHz provide more spectrum than 2.4 GHz**.

---

### Wi-Fi Generations

Know the relationship between the IEEE standard and the Wi-Fi generation:

```text
802.11ac  → Wi-Fi 5
802.11ax  → Wi-Fi 6
802.11ax  → Wi-Fi 6E
802.11be  → Wi-Fi 7
```

**Wi-Fi 6E** is an extension of Wi-Fi 6 that adds access to the **6 GHz band**.

## Exam Notes

### High-Priority Wi-Fi Facts

-   **802.11** = IEEE wireless LAN standards

-   **802.11ac** = Wi-Fi 5

-   **802.11ax** = Wi-Fi 6

-   **Wi-Fi 6E** = Wi-Fi 6 extended into the 6 GHz band

-   **802.11be** = Wi-Fi 7

-   Common Wi-Fi bands: **2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, 6 GHz**

-   Common channel bandwidths: **20, 40, 80, 160 MHz**

-   **2.4 GHz** has less available spectrum and is more susceptible to congestion/interference

-   **5 GHz** provides more available spectrum

-   **6 GHz** provides even more available spectrum

### High-Priority Bluetooth Facts

-   Bluetooth operates in the **2.4 GHz** range.

-   Bluetooth is commonly used for **personal peripherals**.

-   Typical consumer Bluetooth range is about **10 meters**.

-   Bluetooth uses the **unlicensed ISM band**.

### High-Priority RFID Facts

-   RFID = **Radio Frequency Identification**

-   Used for **identification and tracking**

-   Passive RFID tags do **not** have their own battery.

-   Passive tags receive energy from the **RF signal of the scanner**.

-   Active RFID tags have an **internal battery**.

-   Active RFID can operate over a **longer distance**.

### High-Priority NFC Facts

-   NFC = **Near-Field Communication**

-   Builds on RFID technology.

-   Supports **two-way communication**.

-   Common in smartphones and smartwatches.

-   Common uses:

    -   Contactless payments

    -   Identification

    -   Access control

    -   Device configuration
